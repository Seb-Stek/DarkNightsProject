Flip the Switch

Most of the light over a street isn't keeping anyone safe. It's a sign nobody reads, a floodlight aimed at the sky, a porch light left on for an empty yard.

A Sky Fading in One Childhood

Volunteers' star counts show the night sky getting about 10% brighter every year. That is fast enough to see within a single childhood.

Good Light, Bad Light

We're not asking anyone to live in the dark. The fix is better light: aimed down, only as bright as needed, warm, and off when nobody's there.

  1. 01

    Useful

    Only light what someone actually needs to see.

  2. 02

    Targeted

    Aim it down and shield it, so it lands where it's needed.

  3. 03

    Low Level

    No brighter than the task. Brighter isn't safer when it blinds.

  4. 04

    Controlled

    Timers and motion sensors turn it off when nobody's there.

  5. 05

    Warm

    Amber and warm white, 3000K or lower, instead of cold blue-white.

The Light Pollution Scale

Astronomers rate sky darkness on the Bortle scale, from 1 (a remote desert) to 9 (a city center). Most people live at 7 or above.

    Measure Your Own Sky

    You don't need equipment to know how dark your sky is. You need Orion and twenty minutes.

    1. 01

      Go outside on a clear, moonless evening between December and March, when Orion is up in both hemispheres. Give your eyes 20 minutes away from screens.

    2. 02

      Find the three stars in a short straight line. That's Orion's Belt. The four bright stars around it make a box.

    3. 03

      Count every star you can see inside the box, not counting the four corners. Belt stars count.

    4. 04

      Write it down, then count again on the next Dark Night. Send both to Globe at Night, a citizen-science project that has collected sky counts since 2006.

        03

        What We Do

        Once a month, whole neighborhoods switch off at the same time and go outside together. In between, we work on making the dark stick.

        How a Dark Night Works

        1. Someone Signs Up Their Area

          A local host registers the night, then talks to neighbors, shop owners, the school and the council.

        2. Lights Go Down at 9 PM

          Porch lights, shop signs, floodlights and empty-office lights go off. Safety lighting stays on.

        3. Everyone Meets Outside

          A park, a field, a flat roof. Bring a blanket. Local amateur astronomers bring telescopes and point things out.

        4. We Count the Stars

          Each person counts the stars inside one constellation. Comparing counts from before and during shows exactly what the switch-off did.

        A Dark Night, Hour by Hour

        The most important part of the evening is the half hour where nothing seems to happen.

        1. 8:30 pm

          People Arrive

          Blankets down, red flashlights handed out, phones turned to night mode.

        2. 8:45 pm

          First Count

          Everyone counts the stars they can see inside one constellation while the lights are still on.

        3. 9:00 pm

          Lights Down

          Porch lights, signs and floodlights across the neighborhood go off together.

        4. 9:00–9:30

          Eyes Adjust

          The sky fills in slowly. Volunteers point out planets and the brightest stars while everyone waits.

        5. 9:30 pm

          Second Count

          Same constellation, same method. The difference is what the switch-off did, and it goes on our sky quality map.

        6. 9:45 pm

          Telescopes

          Lines form at the eyepieces: Saturn's rings and Jupiter's moons when they're up, the Orion Nebula in winter.

        Our Programs

        Red Light Kits
        Every Dark Night hands out red-filtered flashlights. Red light lets people walk around safely without wrecking their night vision.
        Business Partners
        Shops that switch their signs off after closing get a window sticker and a listing on our map.
        Fixture Swaps
        We pay for and fit shielded, warm-colored outdoor lights on homes and community buildings that ask.
        Schools
        A four-week class where kids make their own star charts, then lead their families around the sky on the next Dark Night.
        Sky Quality Map
        Every star count goes into a public map, so a town can see whether its sky is getting darker or brighter.
        Council Toolkit
        Template letters and a sample lighting ordinance for residents who want their streetlights changed.
        04

        Who We Are

        A nonprofit run mostly by volunteers: hosts, amateur astronomers and neighbors who think everyone should be able to see the stars from their own street.

        How It Started

        It began with one street agreeing to switch its lights off for an evening.

        A few neighbors turned off their porch lights, asked the corner shop to dim its sign, and dragged deck chairs into the road. Someone brought a telescope. Kids who had never seen Saturn's rings lined up to look.

        The next month the street next door joined in. Then a school, then a whole town. Dark Nights grew from that idea: light pollution is easy to fix, and the fastest way to convince people is to show them what they've been missing.

        Who's Involved

        Hosts
        Local volunteers who pick a spot, knock on doors and run the night. Most have no astronomy background at all.
        Volunteer Astronomers
        Members of amateur astronomy clubs who bring telescopes and help people find their way around the sky.
        Partners
        Shops, schools, libraries and councils that switch off, lend space or change their lighting for good.
        A Small Core Team
        Posts out host kits, keeps the sky quality map running and helps councils with lighting policy.

        What We Believe

        Light Where It's Needed

        We're not against light. We're against light that points at the sky, spills into bedrooms and burns all night for nobody.

        Local First

        Change sticks when neighbors ask neighbors. Every Dark Night is organized by someone who lives there.

        Measure It

        Star counts turn "it feels darker" into numbers a council can act on.

        Free to Join

        Dark Nights cost nothing to attend. Host kits are free. Donations cover the rest.

        05

        Why Help?

        Light pollution is outdoor light that goes where it isn't needed: up into the sky, across into bedrooms, out over water. It is the only kind of pollution you can stop with a switch.

        The Problem

        80%
        of the world's population lives under a light-polluted sky.Falchi et al., Science Advances, 2016
        1 in 3
        people can no longer see the Milky Way. In North America it is closer to 4 in 5.Falchi et al., 2016
        ~10%
        is how much brighter the night sky gets each year, judging by what volunteers can still see.Kyba et al., Science, 2023
        ~30%
        of outdoor lighting in the U.S. is wasted, mostly by fixtures that throw light upward.DarkSky International

        What It Harms

        Wildlife

        Sea turtle hatchlings head for streetlights instead of the sea. Migrating birds circle lit towers until they're exhausted or hit the glass.

        Sleep

        Light at night suppresses melatonin. Streetlight through a bedroom window is enough to shift a body clock.

        Energy

        A light pointed at the sky is electricity spent on nothing. Shielding and timers cut it without making streets darker.

        The Sky Itself

        People used the stars to navigate, keep calendars and tell stories for as long as there have been people. Most children now grow up without them.

        About

        Dark Nights Project is a nonprofit working to bring the night sky back to the places people live, starting one neighborhood and one evening at a time.

        Our Mission

        Make it normal for anyone, anywhere, to step outside and see the stars.

        We do that in three ways. We organize Dark Nights so people can see for themselves what a darker sky looks like. We help homes, businesses and councils change their outdoor lighting so the difference lasts. And we collect star counts so every town can measure its progress.

        We don't campaign for darker streets. Good lighting points down, is only as bright as it needs to be, and switches off when nobody's there. That's safer, cheaper and better for everyone.

        Dark Nights

        Is it safe to turn the lights off?

        Yes. Streetlights, stairwell lights and anything needed for safety stay on. We ask people to switch off light that isn't doing anything useful, like empty-office lights, shop signs after closing and floodlights on buildings. Hosts hand out red flashlights so people can walk around safely.

        Why are Dark Nights always near the new moon?

        A full moon is bright enough to wash out faint stars on its own. Around the new moon the sky is as dark as it gets, so the difference from switching lights off is easiest to see.

        What happens if it's cloudy?

        The switch-off still goes ahead, since it still saves energy and gives wildlife a break. The host decides whether to hold the gathering or move it to the following night.

        Do I need a telescope or any astronomy knowledge?

        No. Most of what you'll see needs nothing but your eyes and twenty minutes for them to adjust. Volunteer astronomers often bring telescopes and are happy to show people around.

        Does it cost anything to attend?

        No. Dark Nights are always free, and so are host kits.

        Hosting and Taking Part

        What if my neighbors don't join in?

        Every light that goes off helps, and a gathering spot away from the brightest lights makes a big difference by itself. Many hosts start with a few households and grow from there.

        How do I host a Dark Night?

        Fill in the form on the Locations page. We'll mail you a host kit and try to pair you with a local astronomy club.

        Can businesses and councils take part?

        Yes, and they make the biggest difference. Businesses can join our partner program by switching signs off after hours. Councils can dim or switch off non-essential lighting for the night, and we can help with permanent lighting changes afterwards.

        Impact and Donations

        Does one night a month actually help?

        One night on its own is mostly about showing people what they're missing. The lasting change comes afterwards, when people swap to shielded lights, put lights on timers and ask their councils to do the same. Star counts let us measure whether that's happening.

        Isn't light pollution permanent?

        No. Unlike most pollution, it disappears the moment the light goes off. That's what makes it one of the easiest environmental problems to fix.

        Where does my donation go?

        Mostly into red flashlights, host kits, shielded light fixtures and the schools program. The rest covers the small staff team and running the sky quality map.
