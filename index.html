The light pollution scale

Astronomers rate sky darkness on the Bortle scale, from 1 (a remote desert) to 9 (a city centre). Most people live at 7 or above.

      Host a Dark Night

      Don't see your town? You don't need a telescope or any astronomy knowledge, only a place people can gather and some curious neighbours.

      We'll post you a host kit: flyers, twenty red torches, printed star charts for the season, and a one-page script for asking shops and the council to join in. We'll also try to pair you with an astronomy club nearby.

      03

      What we do

      Once a month, whole neighbourhoods switch off at the same time and go outside together. In between, we work on making the dark stick.

      How a Dark Night works

      1. Someone signs up their area

        A local host registers the night, then talks to neighbours, shop owners, the school and the council.

      2. Lights go down at 9 pm

        Porch lights, shop signs, floodlights and empty-office lights go off. Safety lighting stays on.

      3. Everyone meets outside

        A park, a field, a flat roof. Bring a blanket. Local amateur astronomers bring telescopes and point things out.

      4. We count the stars

        Each person counts the stars inside one constellation. Comparing counts from before and during shows exactly what the switch-off did.

      Our programs

      Red light kits
      Every Dark Night hands out red-filtered torches. Red light lets people walk around safely without wrecking their night vision.
      Business partners
      Shops that switch their signs off after closing get a window sticker and a listing on our map.
      Fixture swaps
      We pay for and fit shielded, warm-coloured outdoor lights on homes and community buildings that ask.
      Schools
      A four-week class where kids make their own star charts, then lead their families around the sky on the next Dark Night.
      Sky quality map
      Every star count goes into a public map, so a town can see whether its sky is getting darker or brighter.
      Council toolkit
      Template letters and a sample lighting ordinance for residents who want their streetlights changed.
      04

      Who we are

      A nonprofit run mostly by volunteers: hosts, amateur astronomers and neighbours who think everyone should be able to see the stars from their own street.

      How it started

      It began with one street agreeing to switch its lights off for an evening.

      A few neighbours turned off their porch lights, asked the corner shop to dim its sign, and dragged deck chairs into the road. Someone brought a telescope. Kids who had never seen Saturn's rings queued up to look.

      The next month the street next door joined in. Then a school, then a whole town. Dark Nights grew from that idea: light pollution is easy to fix, and the fastest way to convince people is to show them what they've been missing.

      Who's involved

      Hosts
      Local volunteers who pick a spot, knock on doors and run the night. Most have no astronomy background at all.
      Volunteer astronomers
      Members of amateur astronomy clubs who bring telescopes and help people find their way around the sky.
      Partners
      Shops, schools, libraries and councils that switch off, lend space or change their lighting for good.
      A small core team
      Posts out host kits, keeps the sky quality map running and helps councils with lighting policy.

      What we believe

      Light where it's needed

      We're not against light. We're against light that points at the sky, spills into bedrooms and burns all night for nobody.

      Local first

      Change sticks when neighbours ask neighbours. Every Dark Night is organized by someone who lives there.

      Measure it

      Star counts turn "it feels darker" into numbers a council can act on.

      Free to join

      Dark Nights cost nothing to attend. Host kits are free. Donations cover the rest.

      05

      Why you should help

      Light pollution is outdoor light that goes where it isn't needed: up into the sky, across into bedrooms, out over water. It is the only kind of pollution you can stop with a switch.

      The problem

      80%
      of the world's population lives under a light-polluted sky.Falchi et al., Science Advances, 2016
      1 in 3
      people can no longer see the Milky Way. In North America it is closer to 4 in 5.Falchi et al., 2016
      ~10%
      is how much brighter the night sky gets each year, judging by what volunteers can still see.Kyba et al., Science, 2023
      ~30%
      of outdoor lighting in the U.S. is wasted, mostly by fixtures that throw light upward.DarkSky International

      What it harms

      Wildlife

      Sea turtle hatchlings head for streetlights instead of the sea. Migrating birds circle lit towers until they're exhausted or hit the glass.

      Sleep

      Light at night suppresses melatonin. Streetlight through a bedroom window is enough to shift a body clock.

      Energy

      A light pointed at the sky is electricity spent on nothing. Shielding and timers cut it without making streets darker.

      The sky itself

      People used the stars to navigate, keep calendars and tell stories for as long as there have been people. Most children now grow up without them.
